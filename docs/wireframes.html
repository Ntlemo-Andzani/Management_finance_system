Microfinance Management System — Wireframes

Low-fidelity layouts for the nine screens and panels in the delivered application. These were drawn before the interface was built and describe structure, hierarchy and control placement only; colour, typography and final copy are settled in the implementation.

Grey blocks — content regions Outlined boxes — inputs and controls Zig-zag lines — placeholder text Numbered markers — see the notes under each frame

W1 — Sign in

Entry point for all four roles. A split layout: identity and context on the left, the form on the right. Demo credentials are listed during development and removed for production.

Notes
  1. Brand panel carries the system name and three portfolio figures — orientation, not decoration.
  2. Failed sign-in returns one message for both a wrong email and a wrong password, so the form cannot be used to discover valid accounts.
  3. Tap-to-fill demo credentials; this block is deleted before live deployment.

W2 — Dashboard

Landing screen after sign in. Answers three questions at a glance: what is the portfolio worth, what is at risk, and what is waiting for a decision.

Notes
  1. Navigation rail is generated from the signed-in role — a teller never sees the staff management link.
  2. Two shortcuts to the highest-frequency actions: take a payment, capture an application.
  3. Five metrics. Portfolio at risk carries a warning accent; the pending queue carries an attention accent.
  4. Six-month disbursement trend beside a fourteen-day collection trend, so lending and recovery are read together.
  5. Ten worst arrears with the responsible officer named, forming the day's call list.

W3 — Client register

Searchable list with a status filter. Selecting a row opens the full profile; the primary action opens the registration form as an overlay so the officer never loses their place in the list.

Notes
  1. Search is debounced and matches name, client number, phone or ID number in one field — a teller has one box to think about.
  2. Status is shown as a coloured pill so a blacklisted client is visible while scanning.
  3. The whole row is the click target; it opens the profile overlay showing loans and savings together.

W4 — Loan application and quote

The most important screen in the system. Form on the left, live quote on the right: the officer prices the loan and shows the client the full schedule before the application is submitted.

Notes
  1. Selecting a product prints its limits underneath — amount range, term range, frequency, fee and penalty rate — so the officer sees the rules before typing figures.
  2. Pricing is a separate, non-committal action. It calls the same calculation engine the disbursement will later use, so the quote and the final schedule cannot disagree.
  3. Instalment, total interest, processing fee, total payable, first due date and maturity date.
  4. Full instalment-by-instalment preview, rendered in the same passbook component used on the live loan.
  5. Submitting only captures the application; no money moves and no schedule is written until a manager approves and disburses.

W5 — Loan detail and repayment schedule

One loan in full. The signature element is the passbook: each instalment is a row with a status rail down its left edge and a stamped state, so arrears are visible without reading a single number.

Notes
  1. A single progress meter reads repayment position faster than any table of figures.
  2. Available actions are computed from loan status and the viewer's role: a manager sees Approve on a pending loan, an officer sees nothing, and a settled loan offers no actions at all.
  3. The passbook. Status rail colours: settled, overdue, part paid, not yet due. Each row carries a stamped state at the right so the schedule is legible in a printout too.

W6 — Repayment counter

Built for speed at a busy counter: select the loan, read the amount due, take the money, hand over the receipt. The amount field is pre-filled with what is actually owed today.

Notes
  1. The loan picker shows the loan number, client name and outstanding balance in one line, so the teller can confirm the client against what they are told at the window.
  2. Three figures the client actually asks for: what is due today (including any penalty), what it would cost to settle in full, and when the next instalment falls.
  3. Amount is pre-filled with the amount due but stays editable — part payments are normal in microfinance and must never be blocked.
  4. The receipt renders immediately after posting, showing how the money was split across penalty, interest and principal, and the new balance.

W7 — Reports

Stacked report sections on one printable page: arrears register, the week's collection list, officer performance, product mix, queued client messages and (for administrators) the audit trail.

Notes
  1. Arrears are sorted by days overdue, worst first, with a coloured ageing pill; the section header totals the amount overdue.
  2. The forward-looking collection list carries client phone numbers so it can be worked straight from the screen.
  3. Officer performance is hidden from officers and tellers — they see a "restricted" message rather than a broken table. The audit trail panel below (not shown) renders for administrators only.

W8 — Credit life

The insurance book. Claims sit above policies because a claim is time-critical and a policy list is reference material. Each row shows cover now, which for a declining policy is the loan balance rather than the figure written at inception.

Notes
  1. Four figures an insurer actually manages by: cover in force, premiums written, claims open, claims paid. Open claims carry the attention accent because they are the only one with a deadline attached.
  2. Claims first, and the available action is computed from status and role together — a manager sees Assess on a lodged claim, only an administrator sees Settle, because settling writes down a loan and pays money out.
  3. The basis column names the cover method, and "cover now" is computed rather than stored: a declining policy shows the loan balance, a flat policy the figure written at inception. Putting them in one column makes the difference between the two products visible at a glance.

W9 — Nominated beneficiaries (panel on the client profile)

Not a screen of its own: nominations belong to a client, so they live at the foot of the client profile. The share bar is the whole point of the panel — an incomplete nomination is a payout that cannot be split, so it has to be impossible to miss.

Notes
  1. The bar turns to the warning colour below 100% and names the shortfall in words underneath, because "69% allocated" means nothing to a teller unless the consequence is spelled out.
  2. A nomination is withdrawn, never deleted. A claim assessed years later has to be able to show who was nominated at the time, so the row is retired instead.
  3. The share field is pre-filled with exactly what is still unallocated and capped there, so the common case is one click and an over-allocation cannot be typed in the first place. The whole form disappears once the nomination reaches 100%.
